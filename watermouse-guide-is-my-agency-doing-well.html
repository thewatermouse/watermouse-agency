How do I know if my Google Ads agency is doing a good job?

By Rodrigo Kato7 min readUpdated September 2026
The short answer

Judge your agency on profit and honesty, not activity. Five checks: are the numbers that matter (revenue, ROAS vs break-even, CPA, new-customer share) moving the right way; does the reporting explain why; is a senior person actually on the account; are they proactive; and, the real test, do they ever tell you to spend less? A long list of “optimisations” is not evidence of a good agency.

It is a genuinely hard thing to judge from the outside. The reports look busy, the charts go up and down, and unless you live in the account you cannot easily tell competence from motion. So here is a framework you can apply without being a Google Ads expert yourself, built around outcomes and behaviour rather than jargon.

1. Judge outcomes, not activity

The most common trick, often unintentional, is to report activity as if it were results: “we made 47 optimisations, added 200 keywords, refreshed the ads.” None of that tells you whether the business made more money. A good agency leads with outcomes and treats the activity as the footnote. If the headline of every report is how busy they were, you are being sold effort, not performance.

2. The five numbers to actually look at

You do not need to read the whole account. These are the money metrics:

MetricWhat good looks like
Revenue & profit from adsGrowing, or holding while efficiency improves.
ROAS vs break-evenComfortably above your break-even ROAS, with a buffer.
CPA (cost to acquire a customer)Stable or falling relative to what a customer is worth.
New vs returning customersBringing in new customers, not just buying back people who would have returned anyway.
Efficiency as you scaleSpend can grow while staying profitable, not just spend growing.

Impressions, clicks and click-through rate are useful diagnostics for the person running the account, but they are not the scoreboard. If they are the headline, be suspicious.

3. Does the reporting explain why?

Anyone can send a dashboard. The value is in the interpretation. A good report tells you why the numbers moved (“ROAS dipped because we scaled into non-brand, and here is why that is still profitable”) and what they are doing about it. If your reports are a wall of numbers with no narrative, you are not getting the expensive part of the service.

4. Who is actually on your account?

This is the quiet one that decides everything. Many agencies sell you a senior name and hand the day-to-day to a junior learning on your budget. Ask directly: who makes the daily decisions on my account, and how many other accounts do they run? You want the person touching the bids and the feed to have run accounts like yours before. It is one of the main reasons some people choose a senior freelancer or solo operator over a traditional agency.

5. Are they proactive, or just reactive?

A good partner brings you ideas: a new market to test, a feed problem they spotted, a structure change worth trying. A weak one only responds when you chase, and only reports what already happened. If every improvement in the last year was something you suggested, you are paying for a pair of hands, not expertise.

The real test: do they ever tell you to spend less?

This is the single most revealing question, and it is why the pricing model matters. An agency paid a percentage of your spend has a quiet incentive to keep spend high. The ones worth keeping will, at some point, tell you to pull back, that a channel is over-invested, that you are past your profitable ceiling, that this month the right move is to spend less. An agency that has never once advised restraint is either very lucky or not really optimising for your profit.

The gut check: a good agency makes you feel like they are spending your money as if it were their own, and occasionally tells you the uncomfortable truth. If yours only ever recommends doing more, faster, for a bigger budget, that is worth a hard look.

Red flags, in one list

  • Reports built on impressions, clicks and “optimisations made.”
  • You do not own, or cannot fully see, your own Google Ads and Merchant Center accounts.
  • A senior name sold you the account; a junior runs it.
  • Long lock-in contracts with heavy exit penalties.
  • A fee that scales up with spend but never down.
  • They have never once told you to pull back or that something is not working.

Frequently asked

How do I know if my Google Ads agency is doing a good job?

Judge them on outcomes and honesty, not activity. Are the numbers that matter (revenue, ROAS versus your break-even, customer acquisition cost, and the share of new customers) moving the right way? Does the reporting explain why they moved, not just what happened? Is a senior person actually on your account? Are they proactive with ideas? And, the real test, do they ever tell you to spend less or that you are over-invested? Good agencies pass all five; the number of optimisations they list is irrelevant.

What metrics should I check to judge my Google Ads agency?

The ones tied to money, not vanity. Revenue and profit from ads, ROAS measured against your break-even ROAS, customer acquisition cost (CPA), the share of new versus returning customers, and how efficiently spend converts as it scales. Impressions, clicks and click-through rate are diagnostics, not scoreboard metrics. If a report leads with impressions and optimisations made, that is a warning sign.

What are red flags in a Google Ads agency?

Reporting built on vanity metrics; not owning or having full access to your own account; a senior name on the contract but a junior actually running the work; long lock-in contracts with heavy exit penalties; a fee that scales up with spend but never down; and an agency that never once tells you to pull back or that something is not working. Any of these is worth a hard conversation.

Should I have access to my own Google Ads account?

Yes, always. You should own the Google Ads and Merchant Center accounts and grant the agency access, not the other way around. If the agency owns the account and you cannot see inside it or take it with you when you leave, that is a serious red flag. Ownership of your own data and history is non-negotiable.

How often should I hear from my Google Ads agency?

There is no single right cadence, but you should get regular, readable reporting and be able to reach the person who actually runs the account. What matters more than frequency is quality: proactive updates when something changes, clear explanations of why the numbers moved, and ideas brought to you rather than only answers when you chase. Silence between monthly dashboards is not a good sign.
